# The 154 Most Important Composers of the Symphony

A chronological survey (by birth year) of the composers who built, expanded, and
sustained the symphony from its origins in the 1730s to the present day.
Composers who never meaningfully contributed to the genre (e.g. Handel, J. S.
Bach, Vivaldi, Bartók) are deliberately excluded.

## Origins & the Classical Era (the medium is born)

1. **Giovanni Battista Sammartini** (1700–1775) — Pioneered the early three-movement Italian symphony; the bridge from Baroque sinfonia to the Classical symphony.
2. **Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach** (1714–1788) — Hamburg and string symphonies of *Empfindsamkeit* expressivity and formal daring.
3. **Johann Stamitz** (1717–1757) — Leader of the Mannheim school; established the four-movement form and the orchestral crescendo.
4. **Joseph Haydn** (1732–1809) — "Father of the Symphony"; 106 works that codified Classical form and dramatic argument.
5. **François-Joseph Gossec** (1734–1829) — Central to the early French symphony and the symphonie concertante.
6. **Johann Christian Bach** (1735–1782) — The "London" Bach; galant symphonies that shaped the young Mozart.
7. **Michael Haydn** (1737–1806) — Some 40 symphonies admired by Mozart; one long misattributed to him as Mozart's "No. 37."
8. **Carl Ditters von Dittersdorf** (1739–1799) — Prolific Viennese contemporary of Haydn; programmatic symphonies after Ovid.
9. **Luigi Boccherini** (1743–1805) — Distinctive symphonist extending the Italian–Classical line.
10. **Carl Stamitz** (1745–1801) — Carried the Mannheim symphonic tradition across Europe.
11. **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart** (1756–1791) — Roughly 41 symphonies culminating in the perfection of nos. 39–41.
12. **Ignaz Pleyel** (1757–1831) — Haydn's pupil; some 40 symphonies that were among the most played in Europe c. 1800.

## Classical into Romantic

13. **Luigi Cherubini** (1760–1842) — His Symphony in D and orchestral mastery influenced Beethoven's generation.
14. **Étienne Méhul** (1763–1817) — French Revolutionary-era symphonist admired by Berlioz.
15. **Ludwig van Beethoven** (1770–1827) — Nine symphonies that made the genre a vehicle of philosophical ambition.
16. **George Onslow** (1784–1853) — The "French Beethoven"; four symphonies central to the French Romantic line.
17. **Louis Spohr** (1784–1859) — Early Romantic and programmatic symphonist ("Die Weihe der Töne").
18. **Franz Berwald** (1796–1868) — Sweden's great Romantic original; four boldly individual symphonies.
19. **Franz Schubert** (1797–1828) — The "Unfinished" and "Great" C major opened the lyrical Romantic symphony.

## The Romantic Symphony

20. **Hector Berlioz** (1803–1869) — *Symphonie fantastique* and *Harold en Italie* invented the program symphony.
21. **Felix Mendelssohn** (1809–1847) — The "Italian," "Scottish," and "Reformation" symphonies restored Classical clarity.
22. **Robert Schumann** (1810–1856) — Four symphonies central to German Romantic symphonism.
23. **Franz Liszt** (1811–1886) — The *Faust* and *Dante* symphonies and the symphonic poem reshaped orchestral form.
24. **Niels Gade** (1817–1890) — Founder of the Scandinavian symphonic tradition.
25. **Joachim Raff** (1822–1882) — Hugely popular in his day; bridged programmatic and absolute symphony.
26. **César Franck** (1822–1890) — His Symphony in D minor became a cornerstone of the French repertoire.
27. **Édouard Lalo** (1823–1892) — His Symphony in G minor is a staple of French Romantic symphonism.
28. **Anton Bruckner** (1824–1896) — Monumental, cathedral-like symphonies that redefined scale and time.
29. **Anton Rubinstein** (1829–1894) — Six symphonies, including the popular "Ocean," that anchored Russian symphonism.
30. **Alexander Borodin** (1833–1887) — Leading symphonist of the Russian nationalist "Mighty Handful."
31. **Johannes Brahms** (1833–1897) — Four symphonies that proved the genre's vitality after Beethoven.
32. **Camille Saint-Saëns** (1835–1921) — The "Organ" Symphony No. 3 is a pillar of French Romantic symphonism.
33. **Felix Draeseke** (1835–1913) — Four substantial symphonies of the German post-Lisztian school.
34. **Mily Balakirev** (1837–1910) — Guiding figure of Russian nationalist orchestral music.
35. **Georges Bizet** (1838–1875) — His youthful Symphony in C is a Classical-Romantic gem.
36. **Max Bruch** (1838–1920) — Three symphonies alongside his famous concertante works.
37. **John Knowles Paine** (1839–1906) — The first significant American symphonist.
38. **Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky** (1840–1893) — Six symphonies (esp. 4–6) brought emotional intensity to the form.
39. **Antonín Dvořák** (1841–1904) — Nine symphonies, including the epochal "New World."
40. **Hubert Parry** (1848–1918) — Five symphonies foundational to the English musical renaissance.
41. **Zdeněk Fibich** (1850–1900) — Three symphonies central to the Czech Romantic tradition.

## Late Romantic & Nationalist

42. **Vincent d'Indy** (1851–1931) — Franckian symphonist and influential pedagogue.
43. **Charles Villiers Stanford** (1852–1924) — Seven symphonies; a pillar of the British revival.
44. **George Whitefield Chadwick** (1854–1931) — Leading symphonist of the "Boston School."
45. **Ernest Chausson** (1855–1899) — His single Symphony in B-flat is a late-Romantic French landmark.
46. **Sergei Taneyev** (1856–1915) — Russian master of contrapuntal symphonic structure.
47. **Edward Elgar** (1857–1934) — His two symphonies revitalized the English symphonic tradition.
48. **Hans Rott** (1858–1884) — His sole Symphony in E major astonishingly prefigures Mahler; a tragic lost pioneer.
49. **Gustav Mahler** (1860–1911) — Ten symphonies that pushed the genre to its expressive and structural limits.
50. **Richard Strauss** (1864–1949) — From early symphonies to the *Symphonia Domestica* and *Alpine Symphony*.
51. **Albéric Magnard** (1865–1914) — Four rigorous, Brucknerian-Franckian symphonies.
52. **Alexander Glazunov** (1865–1936) — Eight symphonies fusing Russian nationalism and Germanic form.
53. **Carl Nielsen** (1865–1931) — Six symphonies notable for "progressive tonality" and organic conflict.
54. **Jean Sibelius** (1865–1957) — Seven symphonies that reinvented form, organicism, and the Nordic voice.
55. **Paul Dukas** (1865–1935) — His Symphony in C is a finely wrought French Romantic statement.
56. **Amy Beach** (1867–1944) — Her "Gaelic" Symphony was the first by an American woman.
57. **Albert Roussel** (1869–1937) — Four symphonies marking a major French modernist voice.
58. **Wilhelm Stenhammar** (1871–1927) — Leading Swedish symphonist of his generation.
59. **Alexander Zemlinsky** (1871–1942) — The *Lyric Symphony* extended the post-Mahlerian song-symphony.
60. **Ralph Vaughan Williams** (1872–1958) — Nine symphonies spanning pastoral, visionary, and modernist idioms.
61. **Sergei Rachmaninoff** (1873–1943) — Three symphonies in the grand late-Romantic Russian manner.
62. **Charles Ives** (1874–1954) — Four radically original symphonies foreshadowing American modernism.
63. **Franz Schmidt** (1874–1939) — Four late-Romantic Austrian symphonies of growing reputation.
64. **Arnold Schoenberg** (1874–1951) — The two Chamber Symphonies reconceived symphonic argument.
65. **Josef Suk** (1874–1935) — The "Asrael" Symphony is a towering Czech late-Romantic threnody.
66. **Havergal Brian** (1876–1972) — 32 symphonies, including the colossal "Gothic."
67. **Ernő Dohnányi** (1877–1960) — Two symphonies in a rich late-Romantic Hungarian idiom.

## Early Twentieth Century & Modernism

68. **Nikolai Myaskovsky** (1881–1950) — 27 symphonies; the central Soviet symphonist of his era.
69. **Karol Szymanowski** (1882–1937) — Four symphonies tracing Polish modernism's evolution.
70. **Igor Stravinsky** (1882–1971) — *Symphony of Psalms*, *Symphony in C*, *Symphony in Three Movements*.
71. **Zoltán Kodály** (1882–1967) — His Symphony in C crowned a defining Hungarian career.
72. **Gian Francesco Malipiero** (1882–1973) — Eleven symphonies central to the Italian instrumental revival.
73. **Anton Webern** (1883–1945) — The Symphony, Op. 21 distilled symphonic thought into serial concision.
74. **Alfredo Casella** (1883–1947) — Three symphonies pivotal to twentieth-century Italian symphonism.
75. **Arnold Bax** (1883–1953) — Seven richly Romantic symphonies of the British school.
76. **Wilhelm Furtwängler** (1886–1954) — Three large-scale symphonies in the Brucknerian tradition.
77. **Kurt Atterberg** (1887–1974) — Nine symphonies anchoring the Swedish Romantic-symphonic line.
78. **Heitor Villa-Lobos** (1887–1959) — Twelve symphonies central to Latin American symphonism.
79. **Florence Price** (1887–1953) — The first symphony by an African-American woman to be performed by a major orchestra.
80. **Bohuslav Martinů** (1890–1959) — Six symphonies of vivid, neo-Baroque energy.
81. **Sergei Prokofiev** (1891–1953) — Seven symphonies from the "Classical" to the monumental Fifth.
82. **Arthur Bliss** (1891–1975) — "A Colour Symphony" and the choral "Morning Heroes."
83. **Arthur Honegger** (1892–1955) — Five symphonies, including the wartime "Liturgique."
84. **Darius Milhaud** (1892–1974) — Twelve numbered symphonies plus chamber symphonies.
85. **Rued Langgaard** (1893–1952) — Sixteen visionary, eccentric Danish symphonies.
86. **E. J. Moeran** (1894–1950) — His Symphony in G minor is a pastoral-British landmark.
87. **Walter Piston** (1894–1976) — Eight symphonies central to American neoclassicism.
88. **Paul Hindemith** (1895–1963) — *Mathis der Maler*, *Symphony in E-flat*, *Die Harmonie der Welt*.
89. **William Grant Still** (1895–1978) — The "Afro-American" Symphony broke new ground in American music.
90. **Howard Hanson** (1896–1981) — Seven symphonies defining an American neo-Romantic voice.
91. **Roberto Gerhard** (1896–1970) — Four symphonies marrying Schoenbergian method and Catalan color.
92. **Roger Sessions** (1896–1985) — Nine symphonies of dense, uncompromising American modernism.
93. **Harald Sæverud** (1897–1992) — Nine symphonies central to the Norwegian tradition.
94. **Roy Harris** (1898–1979) — His Third Symphony is a landmark of American symphonism.
95. **Carlos Chávez** (1899–1978) — Six symphonies foundational to Mexican concert music.
96. **Jón Leifs** (1899–1968) — Rugged, elemental symphonic music from Iceland.
97. **Aaron Copland** (1900–1990) — The Third Symphony crowned the American populist symphony.
98. **Edmund Rubbra** (1901–1986) — Eleven symphonies of contrapuntal, organic growth.
99. **William Walton** (1902–1983) — Two symphonies central to twentieth-century British music.
100. **Aram Khachaturian** (1903–1978) — Colorful Soviet-Armenian symphonist.
101. **Michael Tippett** (1905–1998) — Four symphonies of visionary post-tonal complexity.
102. **Karl Amadeus Hartmann** (1905–1963) — Eight symphonies of intense expressionist weight.

## Mid-Century

103. **Dmitri Shostakovich** (1906–1975) — Fifteen symphonies; the defining symphonic cycle of the century.
104. **Olivier Messiaen** (1908–1992) — The vast *Turangalîla-Symphonie* redefined symphonic scale and color.
105. **Elliott Carter** (1908–2012) — The Symphony of Three Orchestras epitomized American high modernism.
106. **Vagn Holmboe** (1909–1996) — Thirteen symphonies built on his "metamorphosis" technique.
107. **Grażyna Bacewicz** (1909–1969) — Four symphonies from a leading Polish modernist.
108. **Samuel Barber** (1910–1981) — Two symphonies within his lyrical American idiom.
109. **William Schuman** (1910–1992) — Ten symphonies central to mid-century American music.
110. **Allan Pettersson** (1911–1980) — Seventeen symphonies of unbroken, anguished intensity.
111. **Benjamin Britten** (1913–1976) — *Sinfonia da Requiem*, *Spring Symphony*, *Simple Symphony*.
112. **Witold Lutosławski** (1913–1994) — Four symphonies charting postwar avant-garde innovation.
113. **Andrzej Panufnik** (1914–1991) — Ten symphonies of geometric, ritualistic design.
114. **David Diamond** (1915–2005) — Eleven symphonies in a lyrical American neo-Romantic style.
115. **Vincent Persichetti** (1915–1987) — Nine symphonies, including the landmark Sixth for band.
116. **Henri Dutilleux** (1916–2013) — Two refined symphonies central to postwar French music.
117. **Isang Yun** (1917–1995) — Five symphonies fusing Korean aesthetics and European modernism.
118. **Leonard Bernstein** (1918–1990) — Three symphonies ("Jeremiah," "The Age of Anxiety," "Kaddish").
119. **George Rochberg** (1918–2005) — Six symphonies tracing his break from serialism to neo-tonality.
120. **Galina Ustvolskaya** (1919–2006) — Five severe, uncompromising symphonies of stark spiritual force.
121. **Mieczysław Weinberg** (1919–1996) — Twenty-six symphonies; a major rediscovered Soviet voice.
122. **Malcolm Arnold** (1921–2006) — Nine symphonies of vivid, often dark British craftsmanship.
123. **Robert Simpson** (1921–1997) — Eleven symphonies and a lifelong champion of the genre.
124. **Ruth Gipps** (1921–1999) — Five symphonies; a combative anti-modernist now widely revived and recorded.
125. **Peter Mennin** (1923–1983) — Nine taut, energetic American symphonies.
126. **Joly Braga Santos** (1924–1988) — Six symphonies central to twentieth-century Portuguese music.
127. **Julia Perry** (1924–1979) — An African-American composer of roughly twelve symphonies, long neglected and now being rediscovered.
128. **Luciano Berio** (1925–2003) — *Sinfonia* (1968) reinvented the symphony as collage and commentary.
129. **Hans Werner Henze** (1926–2012) — Ten symphonies spanning lyrical and politically charged idioms.
130. **Einojuhani Rautavaara** (1928–2016) — Eight symphonies, including the mystical "Angel of Light."
131. **Kenneth Leighton** (1929–1988) — Three symphonies, including the choral *Sinfonia mistica*; a leading British contrapuntalist.

## Late Twentieth Century & the Contemporary Symphony

132. **Sofia Gubaidulina** (1931–2024) — A towering late-modern figure; *Stimmen… Verstummen*, a symphony in twelve movements, brings her spiritual intensity to the genre.
133. **Per Nørgård** (1932–2025) — Eight symphonies built on his "infinity series"; a major modern Dane.
134. **Krzysztof Penderecki** (1933–2020) — Eight symphonies marking his path from avant-garde to neo-Romanticism.
135. **Henryk Górecki** (1933–2010) — The Third Symphony ("Sorrowful Songs") became a cultural phenomenon.
136. **Gloria Coates** (1933–2023) — Sixteen symphonies of distinctive glissando-saturated texture.
137. **Alfred Schnittke** (1934–1998) — Nine symphonies built on polystylism and historical collage.
138. **Peter Maxwell Davies** (1934–2016) — Ten symphonies plus ten "Strathclyde" concertos; a prolific Briton.
139. **Giya Kancheli** (1935–2019) — Seven symphonies of stark contrast and meditative stillness.
140. **Aulis Sallinen** (1935–2025) — Eight symphonies central to modern Finnish music.
141. **Arvo Pärt** (b. 1935) — Four symphonies tracing his path to "tintinnabuli" spirituality.
142. **Philip Glass** (b. 1937) — Over fourteen symphonies extending the genre into minimalism.
143. **John Harbison** (b. 1938) — Six symphonies from a leading American composer.
144. **John Corigliano** (b. 1938) — His First Symphony (an AIDS memorial) is among the most performed of recent decades.
145. **Ellen Taaffe Zwilich** (b. 1939) — Five symphonies; first woman to win the Pulitzer Prize for Music.
146. **David Matthews** (b. 1943) — Nine symphonies sustaining the British symphonic line.
147. **John Adams** (b. 1947) — *Harmonielehre*, the "Doctor Atomic Symphony," and *City Noir* in symphonic scope.
148. **Kalevi Aho** (b. 1949) — Seventeen-plus symphonies; among today's most prolific symphonists.
149. **Christopher Rouse** (1949–2019) — Six dramatic, hard-hitting American symphonies.
150. **Carl Vine** (b. 1954) — Eight symphonies central to contemporary Australian music.
151. **Magnus Lindberg** (b. 1958) — Large-scale symphonic works from a leading Finnish modernist.
152. **Esa-Pekka Salonen** (b. 1958) — Symphonic and orchestral works from the composer-conductor.
153. **James MacMillan** (b. 1959) — Five symphonies of spiritual and dramatic intensity.
154. **Aaron Jay Kernis** (b. 1960) — Pulitzer-winning symphonist of the contemporary American scene.
155. **Lera Auerbach** (b. 1973) — A leading symphonist of the current generation, with numbered symphonies including "Chimera" and "Arctica."
